# 01 — From prompting to interaction

[Home](../README.md) · 60 minutes · Introductory · No AI account needed

## Learning objectives

By the end, a learner can map the people, context, system access, and success criteria in an AI-supported task; distinguish a better-worded request from a better-designed interaction; and revise an exchange after identifying a hidden assumption.

## Conceptual framing

A request such as “design a successful workshop” does not establish what success means. Is success attendance, learning, comfort, disagreement, or a decision? It also does not say who gets to decide. Interaction Literacy treats the exchange as situated: participants bring purposes and assumptions, a system operates with available context and capabilities, and other people may bear consequences.

This is the Trivian educational frame, not an experimental finding about improved performance. The practical question is whether it reveals something the learner would otherwise miss. A long prompt may still conceal an inappropriate goal. A brief request may be enough when the context is shared and the stakes are low.

## Key distinctions

- Context is information available in an exchange; it is not a guarantee that it will be interpreted as intended.
- Framing specifies what the problem is; wording specifies how a request is expressed. Either can be revised.
- A system's agreement does not establish that it understands the task in the same way the human does. Ask for an observable work product or explicit assumptions.
- Personalization can fit an output to a preference without establishing mutual responsibility or reliable continuity.

## Worked example

A library educator asks an AI collaborator for an “engaging” digital-literacy session. The response proposes a live account-creation contest. The plan is energetic and clear. However, some attendees do not want accounts; others share devices, need interpretation, or are attending precisely because they distrust digital services.

Changing the request to “make it more inclusive” may help, but leaves inclusion undefined. A stronger revision names the conditions: no account required; printed alternatives; no public disclosure of skill level; success is each attendee identifying one question they can ask before adopting a service. Ask the system to identify which activity meets each condition and what it cannot infer. The educator still needs to review feasibility with the venue and learners.

A legitimate competing view is that optional account creation could be valuable. The question is whether participation, alternatives, and the learning goal make that choice meaningful, not whether accounts are inherently wrong.

## Exercise: map, compare, revise — 25 minutes

Use [the interaction map](../exercises/interaction-map.md). Work alone first, then compare with a partner. Identify at least one stakeholder absent from the original request. Revise the task in no more than 150 words. Swap maps and ask: what would have to be true for this plan to work? Keep unresolved assumptions visible rather than inventing answers.

Offline: evaluate the supplied library scenario. Optional live AI: provide only fictional context, ask for two competing workshop designs, and record what changed when you supplied a missing constraint. Do not infer a universal model property from one trial.

## Reflection — 10 minutes

Which part of your revision changes the goal rather than the language? What did you assume about the learners? Where would direct conversation with a person be more useful than another model response? What outcome would make you revise your approach?

## Facilitator guidance

Allow 10 minutes for framing, 10 for the example, 25 for the activity, 10 for reflection, and 5 for an exit response. Offer written or spoken participation. Do not require public accounts or personal histories. When learners disagree about the library plan, distinguish different goals from different beliefs about facts. Ask each side to name a condition under which the other design would be preferable.

If a learner writes an elaborate prompt without identifying stakeholders, return to the map. If another refuses any AI involvement, invite them to evaluate the institutional decision to introduce it; that remains a valid learning path.

## Assessment possibilities

Collect the original map, revised request, and a three-sentence explanation of the revision. Look for a concrete success criterion, one affected person beyond the requester, an explicit limitation, and a revisable assumption. The quality of reasoning matters more than whether the resulting prose sounds impressive. Apply the [reasoning rubric](../assessments/reasoning-rubric.md).

## Further inquiry

Read [UNESCO's education guidance](../resources/reading.md) as a policy perspective on agency and educational design. Research question: would these maps improve transfer to unfamiliar tasks, compared with instruction focused on prompt wording alone? A useful study would specify both outcomes and possible harms, including extra workload.
