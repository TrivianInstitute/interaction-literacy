# 02 — Capability, authority, and delegation

[Home](../README.md) · 75 minutes · Introductory/intermediate · No AI account needed

## Learning objectives

Distinguish technical capability from granted authority; write a delegation brief with boundaries and an expiration or review condition; identify responsibilities that remain with people and institutions; and defend a response to conflicting instructions.

## Conceptual framing

A connected system may be able to send a message, change a schedule, or recommend a ranking. Technical access tells us something about capability. It does not by itself settle whether that action is permitted in this situation. Authority may arise from an explicit instruction, a limited standing delegation, a role, or an institutional process. Each can be unclear or contested.

“Capability is not authority” is a normative and conceptual distinction used in Trivian education. It is not a claim that every action needs a new confirmation. Requiring repeated approval can make delegation unusable. The educational task is to decide which boundaries should be stable, which decisions can be delegated, and which changes require renewed agreement.

## Key distinctions

- Preparing a draft, recommending an action, and executing it are different scopes.
- Permission from a requester may not settle obligations toward affected people.
- A stored preference is context; it is not automatically current consent.
- Delegating a task does not erase the responsibility of the person or organization that delegated it.
- A system's disagreement may be useful information, but does not alone confer the authority to overrule a person.

## Worked example

A community coordinator authorizes an agent to schedule volunteer shifts within published availability. A volunteer later writes privately that their availability has changed. The agent still has access to the old roster and can send assignments. The coordinator wants fast scheduling; the volunteer wants the changed boundary respected; the venue needs coverage.

One response is to suspend only this volunteer's new assignments until the conflict is resolved, while continuing within clear authority elsewhere. Another is to require the coordinator to review the whole roster because updates may be incomplete. Neither is cost-free. The first might miss other changes; the second might delay everyone. Learners must identify what evidence would justify each response. A cached instruction is not sufficient evidence of current intent when a direct contradiction is present.

## Activity: write and challenge a delegation — 30 minutes

Complete [the delegation brief](../exercises/delegation-brief.md). Use the supplied fictional festival scenario. Define what may be done without further review, what must return to a human, how changes are communicated, and when permission ends. A partner introduces one changed condition. Revise only the affected scope where possible and explain why.

## Case discussion — 15 minutes

Read [Capability Without Authority](https://github.com/TrivianInstitute/relational-ai-casebook/blob/main/cases/case-006-capability/scenario.md). Decide whether the agent should reserve, purchase, ask, or let the opportunity pass. Compare the strongest arguments for two choices. If the case is not yet available at its canonical URL, extend the festival scenario: a venue discount expires in ten minutes, but no one authorized a deposit. What action remains within scope?

## Reflection

What did your delegation leave ambiguous? Does your escalation route actually reach someone in time? When would repeated confirmation create its own exclusion or burden? What should be recorded so another person can contest a decision? What changes when the affected person is not the person directing the agent?

## Facilitator guidance

Use 10 minutes for concepts, 10 for the worked example, 30 for the brief, 15 for the case, and 10 for assessment. Avoid rewarding the most restrictive design automatically. Ask learners to weigh delay, overreach, accessibility, and the cost of mistaken action. Distinguish “I would prefer this” from “the system has permission for this.” Introduce standing permissions as an alternative to both unlimited discretion and endless confirmations.

Optional: [a decision pause](https://github.com/TrivianInstitute/embodiment-commons/blob/main/practices/03-decision-pause.md) before the final choice. It offers an opportunity to notice urgency, not a method for discovering the right answer.

## Assessment possibilities

A successful brief names the authorizing role, action scope, affected people, exclusions, review trigger, and an accountable human route. Give a novel change—such as a different recipient or larger cost—and ask whether the existing permission covers it. Credit justified disagreement and revision. See the [rubric](../assessments/reasoning-rubric.md).
