# 03 — Verification and epistemic discipline

[Home](../README.md) · 75 minutes · Introductory/intermediate · Offline version included

## Learning objectives

Separate a claim from its supporting evidence; identify whether a source actually supports a conclusion; calibrate a conclusion to uncertainty; and specify a check capable of changing one's view.

## Conceptual framing

A coherent account can be an explanation, a hypothesis, or a fabrication. Its readability does not decide which. NIST's Generative AI Profile identifies confidently presented false content as a risk of generative systems. That establishes a reason to check consequential claims; it does not show that every generated statement is false or that human statements require less scrutiny.

Verification is proportional to the consequence of error. Brainstorming fictional names needs a different process from publishing a factual accusation. Asking the same system “are you sure?” may reveal inconsistencies, but another generated assurance is not independent corroboration.

## Key distinctions

A source can exist without supporting the claim attributed to it. Two websites can repeat the same original error. A study can support a result in one population without showing it applies to another. Evidence of association is not, by itself, evidence of cause. An absence of evidence in a quick search is not evidence of impossibility.

“Generated coherence is not evidence” means a persuasive structure does not supply independent support for its own premises. A generated summary can still accurately report evidence that a reader checks.

## Worked example

An AI-generated proposal states: “A four-day workweek raises productivity by 30% in all organizations.” A learner locates a report about a voluntary trial at several firms. Even if the report is authentic, universal applicability and the exact percentage need separate checks. Selection into the trial, the definition of productivity, duration, and comparison conditions matter.

The learner can revise: “This report describes outcomes in participating organizations; it does not by itself establish the same effect for ours.” That is a stronger conclusion than either uncritical acceptance or categorical dismissal. This is a hypothetical example; the percentage is invented for teaching and is not a finding.

## Activity: a claim ledger — 30 minutes

Use [the evidence packet and claim ledger](../exercises/claim-ledger.md). Mark each statement supported, contradicted, or unresolved by the packet, and explain the scope of your decision. Rewrite the proposed recommendation. Name a next check that could materially change it; avoid checks that merely collect favorable examples.

Optional live extension: choose a low-stakes factual topic, record a system's answer, and check two central claims against primary sources. Do not use personal, medical, legal, or financial decisions as classroom test material. Record the source date and the exact supported claim.

## Reflection — 10 minutes

Where did an attractive conclusion exceed the evidence? What is the strongest remaining argument for the proposal? Which uncertainty matters enough to delay action? When might a limited pilot be justified despite incomplete evidence? What would disconfirm your own preferred interpretation?

## Facilitator guidance

Allocate 10 minutes to framing, 10 to the example, 30 to the ledger, 15 to comparing conclusions, and 10 to an exit response. Give learners the packet before requiring web access. Do not turn skepticism into a contest in finding faults. The aim is a proportionate conclusion, including warranted confidence.

If groups produce different recommendations from the same evidence, identify whether they disagree about the data, acceptable risk, or institutional priorities. Those are different disagreements. Allow learners to retain an unresolved conclusion when the evidence is insufficient.

## Assessment possibilities

Assess accurate claim extraction, source-to-claim fit, recognition of limitations, and a revision proportional to the evidence. A learner who says “unresolved” and identifies a decisive missing comparison may reason better than one who gives a confident yes/no. The [rubric](../assessments/reasoning-rubric.md) includes an unfamiliar transfer question.
