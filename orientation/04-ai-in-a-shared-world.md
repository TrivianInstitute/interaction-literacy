# 04 — AI in a shared world

[Pathway](README.md) · [Previous lesson](03-data-design-incentives.md) · [Final transfer task](workbook.md#final-transfer-task--20-minutes) · 30 minutes

**Learning goal:** examine a proposed AI service as a shared arrangement involving people, institutions, work, access, and physical resources; propose an accountable next step.  
**Materials:** this page and a way to respond; no account or real institutional records.  
**Your artifact:** a bounded proposal, including benefits, limits, responsibility, and evidence that could change it.

## Who is part of this decision? — 3 minutes

**Fictional situation:** River House, a community learning center, wants a digital assistant that answers questions about classes. The director hopes it will reduce repeated administrative work. Some learners want answers outside office hours. Staff want time to help with unusual problems. Other residents prefer to speak to a person or need a response format the proposed interface does not yet provide.

List three people or groups whose experience should influence the decision. Include someone who may never interact directly with the assistant. Everything about River House and its proposed service in this lesson is invented.

## Four parts of the wider picture — 7 minutes

### Accountability is an arrangement

Someone needs to own the information, decide the permitted use, respond to problems, and change or stop the arrangement when necessary. “A person reviews it” is incomplete if that person lacks time, relevant knowledge, or permission to intervene. In the Trivian educational frame, affected people should have meaningful ways to question an outcome. This is a normative commitment explored in the [Foundations](../FOUNDATIONS.md), not a finding that every review process works.

### Accessibility concerns the whole route

Digital accessibility includes being able to perceive information, operate an interface, understand it, and use it with relevant assistive technologies. Keyboard access, readable text, adjustable time, and alternatives to inaccessible media are examples. W3C provides technical guidance; a service's claim to be helpful does not establish that it meets a particular person's access needs. [W3C accessibility principles](sources.md#s9-accessibility).

A learner may need assistance, translation, or an alternative channel. Ask whether the route still reaches the same service, whether it imposes extra cost or delay, and how the provider will hear about a barrier. These questions apply to ordinary websites as well as AI interfaces.

### Work changes at the task level

The ILO's 2025 study estimates occupational exposure by examining tasks. Exposure to possible automation is not a measured count of jobs lost. For many jobs, the relevant question is how a combination of tasks could change. [ILO Working Paper 140](sources.md#s10-work).

For River House, ask who will maintain class information, review difficult answers, correct mistakes, and respond to complaints. Those are tasks in this proposal, not invisible work that disappears when an assistant is installed. Staff and learners may reasonably disagree about what should be automated or where saved time should go.

### Digital participation has a material setting

Training and operating models use computing resources. Environmental effects vary with the workload, hardware, and energy supply; an estimate for one system or task should not be applied to every exchange. [NIST's generative-AI profile, section 2.5](sources.md#s7-generative-ai-risks). The IEA examines the electricity infrastructure and supply chains supporting data centers, alongside possible energy-sector benefits from AI. [IEA, Key Questions on Energy and AI](sources.md#s11-energy-and-material-infrastructure).

A shared decision can therefore ask about service scale, equipment, electricity, maintenance, and disposal, while also asking what benefit the service provides. The lesson proposes those questions; it does not assign a footprint to River House or claim that one type of response has a universal resource cost. Provider disclosure and a clear comparison would be needed for a quantified assessment.

## Worked example: make the next step concrete — 4 minutes

A weak proposal says, “Install it because it saves time, and keep a human in the loop.” It states a hoped-for benefit without explaining the work or a review route.

A more concrete proposal could be: “Try a limited class-information assistant that can suggest answers using current, approved course records. Keep phone and front-desk support. Assign a staff owner to update the records and receive corrections. Before expanding, ask whether answers are accurate, whether access routes work, and whether staff time is actually reduced after counting maintenance and repair.”

That is one defensible option. Delaying adoption until the interface is accessible, or using a simpler searchable directory, could also be justified. No trial or benefit has been demonstrated in this fictional example. A proposal becomes stronger by naming the conditions under which it should change.

## Your task: advise River House — 9 minutes

Use these additional fictional facts:

- The proposed service generates answers from course records supplied by River House; it can link to those records.
- It can draft a registration form but cannot submit it or take payment.
- The first prototype requires voice input and does not yet accept typed questions.
- Its provider offers a general efficiency claim without evidence from this center.
- The center has one staff member responsible for the existing class list; no extra review time has been allocated.
- The contract draft does not say how question logs are retained or deleted.
- The provider has not supplied a service-specific resource estimate. The current phone line and printed directory already use staff time and physical resources too.

Propose a next step: adopt a limited version after named changes, conduct a bounded evaluation, postpone, or choose another approach. Explain your choice in five short paragraphs, a table, a diagram with explanation, or a spoken response. Include:

1. **Purpose and benefit:** whose need is being addressed, and what would count as improvement?
2. **Access and participation:** one concrete barrier, an alternative route, and how affected people help evaluate it.
3. **Work and authority:** who owns records and correction, what time they need, and what the service may prepare or do.
4. **Information and resources:** a question about logs and a question about material/resource costs; state which evidence is missing.
5. **Revision:** one condition for proceeding and one condition for changing or stopping the proposal.

Then respond to a new fact: staff report that a prototype answer gives last term's start date, even though the correct current date is in the supplied records. What needs correction now? What additional observation would help determine whether wider use is appropriate?

## Feedback and comparison — 5 minutes

A well-supported proposal should notice that voice-only input creates a barrier for some users. It should request an accessible interaction route and a way to check that it works with people who use it. Maintaining a phone line alone would not resolve every voice-related barrier; a printed or typed route may also matter.

It should retain the distinction between drafting and submitting a registration. A staff owner needs allocated time and a correction process. “AI will free staff” remains a claim to evaluate after counting maintenance, verification, and repair. Question-log retention is unresolved and should be settled before collecting real learners' questions under that arrangement.

The missing resource estimate does not prove either negligible impact or unacceptable impact. A reasonable proposal can ask for scoped information and compare alternatives, without inventing a number or reducing the whole decision to a single metric.

For the wrong date, correct or withdraw the inaccurate answer and check the updated output against the current record. Investigate the source-selection and generation steps before assuming that having the correct record is enough. Further evaluation should use representative questions, include relevant access needs, record errors, and consider the staff work required to address them. This is a proposed evaluation design, not completed evidence.

Different final recommendations can be defensible. Compare whether each proposal answers an actual need, takes an access barrier seriously, assigns responsibility, and explains when it would change. “Always adopt” and “never adopt” require reasoning just as any other position does.

## Exit and facilitation — 2 minutes

Finish: “The people who should be able to change this arrangement include ___, because ___.”

Facilitators: keep this an educational case, not a demand that participants disclose an employer's plans or a personal difficulty. A learner may work privately or ask a scribe to record their judgment. If discussion becomes a broad forecast about AI, return to the supplied facts and distinguish a possibility from an observed outcome.
