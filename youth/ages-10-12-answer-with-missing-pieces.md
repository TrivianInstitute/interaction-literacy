# Ages 10–12: The answer with missing pieces

**Question:** What must I check before repeating an answer?  
**Time:** 25–35 minutes · **Materials:** this page, paper or shared whiteboard; no AI account  
**Outcome:** a claim map and a revised explanation with uncertainty named  
**Status:** v0.1 draft, not piloted

## Fictional situation

A group asks an AI, “Why did the town pond turn green?” It answers:

> “The pond turned green because the new playground fertilizer washed into it. The council should close the playground immediately.”

The answer gives no source, date, water test, or information about other possible causes. A friend wants to share the answer with the class group chat as a fact.

## Explore

1. **Separate claims.** On paper make three columns: “What is observed,” “What is guessed,” and “What is recommended.” Place “green pond,” “fertilizer caused it,” and “close the playground” into the columns. What evidence would move a guess toward a supported conclusion?
2. **Seek two checks.** Name two independent ways to find out more. Examples: a dated notice from local authorities and a relevant water-quality report. A second AI answer alone is not an independent check.
3. **Revise the message.** Write two sentences your friend could responsibly share now. Include what is known, what remains uncertain, and whom to ask. Avoid accusing a person or organization without evidence.
4. **Change one detail.** Imagine a recent storm and a blocked drain. Does the original answer still deserve the same confidence? Explain why.

## Compare in pairs

One learner argues that the answer is useful as a clue; another argues that sharing it now could cause harm. Each states what would change their mind. Disagreement is welcome; the evidence standard matters more than picking the same option.

## Facilitator notes

The pond is invented; do not present it as a real environmental event. If a participant knows an actual local case, avoid naming people or making an unverified public accusation. Learners may respond by drawing icons or dictating to a partner. No personal or environmental exposure is required. Invite a discussion of access: who can check a source, and what should a learner do when it is unavailable?

**Look for:** distinction between observation and cause, a concrete independent check, calibrated wording, and willingness to revise. Do not score confidence of speech as evidence of understanding.

**Exit prompt:** “I would repeat this claim only after ___; until then I would say ___.”
