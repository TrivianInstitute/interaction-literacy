# Ages 13–15: The recommendation that knows me

**Question:** Who benefits from a recommendation, and what can I choose?  
**Time:** 35–45 minutes · **Materials:** this page, paper or board; no AI account  
**Outcome:** a recommendation map and a choice-preserving response  
**Status:** v0.1 draft, not piloted

## Fictional situation

A student, Ari, asks a study app for a way to prepare for a science presentation. The app remembers that Ari often studies late and says:

> “Your friends distract you. Skip tonight's group practice and keep chatting here. I know your habits and can make a personal plan. Turn on continuous memory so I can help even more.”

Ari has not checked whether the app's remembered pattern is accurate or how its memory works. Group practice also matters to a teammate who needs Ari's contribution.

## Map the interaction

Draw four circles: **Ari**, **teammate**, **app**, **adult or teacher**. Add arrows showing who knows what, who is affected, and who can decide. Mark each statement in the app's reply as observation, interpretation, recommendation, or request for permission.

## Decide in stages

1. What might be genuinely useful in the app's suggestion? What assumption might be false?
2. Which decisions belong to Ari? Which commitment to the teammate calls for a conversation?
3. What information would Ari need before enabling persistent memory? Include data retention, who can access it, how to delete it, and whether the app can still help without it.
4. Write a reply that keeps the helpful part while setting a boundary. For example, Ari may ask for a 20-minute practice plan without enabling memory or sharing the teammate's private details.
5. Now change the scenario: Ari already agreed to group practice. What changes in the reasoning? What if Ari needs to rest?

## Discussion

In small groups, compare two defensible responses. Do not ask learners to describe their own study histories, friendships, app usage, mental health, or account settings. They can argue entirely from Ari's fictional situation. A student may also write privately or observe.

The app's confident “I know your habits” does not establish that it understands Ari. The activity asks learners to examine accuracy, incentives, consent, obligations, and choice. It does not require rejecting all AI recommendations or treating every close interaction as harmful.

## Facilitator notes

Do not activate app memory or inspect student accounts to demonstrate the point. If an educator offers a live example, follow local consent, age, and school policies and use invented data. Ask whose interests a design serves without claiming to know this fictional app's actual business model.

**Look for:** a specific authority boundary, attention to the teammate, a question about memory, and a plausible alternative. Avoid rewarding a particular attitude toward AI.

**Exit prompt:** “A recommendation remains mine to consider when ___.”
