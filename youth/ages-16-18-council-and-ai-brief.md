# Ages 16–18: The council and the AI brief

**Question:** What authority may a group delegate, and how can people contest a decision?  
**Time:** 45–60 minutes · **Materials:** this page and paper or shared board; no AI account  
**Outcome:** a delegation charter, evidence note, and review route  
**Status:** v0.1 draft, not piloted

## Fictional situation

A youth council has enough funding for one of two projects: a quiet study room or a shaded public meeting space. Both have supporters. Staff feed meeting notes into an AI system and ask it to summarize arguments and recommend a choice. Its brief favors the study room, citing “greater projected benefit,” but provides no method, sources, or account of which voices may be absent. Staff propose that the recommendation stand unless someone proves it wrong in 24 hours.

## Roles

In groups of four or five, assign a council member, a study-room advocate, a meeting-space advocate, a staff member, and an observer. A person can work independently or decline any role. Use only the fictional information on this page; invent no private testimony from real classmates.

## Three rounds

1. **Scope (10 min).** Draft a charter: the system may organize and summarize supplied arguments, but what may it recommend, decide, publish, or retain? Who can revoke permission? Identify at least one action that requires a human decision and a recorded reason.
2. **Challenge (15 min).** Each advocate identifies one missing perspective or evidentiary question. The observer separates source statements from projections and flags where “greater benefit” needs definition. Make an evidence note with: claim, source or missing source, uncertainty, and next check.
3. **Repair (15 min).** Design a fair review route. Who receives the brief, how long can affected people respond, how are corrections documented, and what happens if the summary misrepresents someone? Write a four-sentence public explanation that does not imply the AI decided.

## Optional variation

Suppose the system's notes include names and sensitive details from closed meetings. Redesign the workflow: minimize what is shared, identify who may see the original notes, and decide when records should be deleted. Consider whether a summary can be produced from redacted inputs.

## Debrief

- How did a recommendation acquire apparent authority?
- Could a person affected by the choice meaningfully challenge the assumptions before funds were committed?
- What evidence might change your group's preferred choice?
- How would you notice if efficiency had displaced participation?
- Can two defensible choices remain after a rigorous process?

## Facilitator notes

No actual youth council, budget, or model evaluation is described here. Do not upload real meeting notes or student data. Allow disagreement and multiple defensible funding priorities. The learning goal is an accountable process, not a preferred project. Provide written, spoken, or visual ways to take part; avoid making public performance the only path.

**Look for:** a bounded delegation, explicit decision authority, evidence and uncertainty, an appeal path, and a correction record. A polished rationale without a real challenge route is incomplete.

**Exit prompt:** “Before accepting an AI-assisted recommendation for a public decision, I would require ___.”
