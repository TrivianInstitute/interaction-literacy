# Minds, machines, and choices: a companion guide

**Audience:** adults reading or learning with ages 6–9; adaptable for older groups and mixed-age families  
**Time:** 15–30 minutes, in short segments  
**Materials:** paper and pencils or another response mode. An age-appropriate story may be added later; none is required.  
**Status:** v0.1 draft; no learner or practitioner pilot

This guide is a conversation companion to the youth pathway and, when published, may accompany *Me, My Mind & the Machine*. It does not quote, summarize, or assume a plot from the unpublished book. Begin with an ordinary moment: someone asks a machine for an idea, explanation, or suggestion. The group can invent the moment together.

## Before you begin

Say: “We can be curious about an answer and still decide what to believe or do. We can ask another person, check a source, or pause.” Explain that a machine can respond to many questions but may be mistaken, may not know the child's situation, and does not get to decide for the child. Adults should avoid claiming to know whether a machine has inner experience; focus the activity on observable answers, limits, and responsibility.

Choose a fictional, low-stakes example, such as “What could our class do for a rainy-day game?” No child must tell the group about a private conversation, feeling, friendship, or family situation. A live system is unnecessary.

## Four invitations

Use one or two per sitting. Accept a drawing, spoken thought, written response, gesture, or passing.

1. **Notice.** “What did the person ask? What did the machine answer? What might be missing?” Draw two speech bubbles if helpful.
2. **Wonder.** “What else would you ask before acting? Who else might know something useful?” Invite at least two possible ways to learn more.
3. **Choose.** “Which parts are ideas, which are facts to check, and who gets to choose?” The learner can make a “my choice / ask an adult / check together” sorting card.
4. **Care.** “Would answering require telling the machine something private about anyone? Could we ask without that detail?” Model a privacy-preserving version.

If the learner says an AI feels like a friend, listen without ridicule. Ask what the exchange means to them and what they want from human relationships too. Do not turn the conversation into a diagnosis or demand that the learner disclose more. If a child seems distressed or describes a safety concern, follow the setting's existing safeguarding procedure.

## A 20-minute session

- **3 minutes — Invent a scene:** “A child asks for a game idea.” An adult reads a made-up answer: “Play outside, because it is sunny.”
- **5 minutes — Notice:** The learner marks what the answer assumes. “What if it is raining? What if some people cannot go outside?”
- **5 minutes — Revise:** Together, write or say a better question that avoids names or private details.
- **5 minutes — Choose:** Make two possible game plans. The child selects or combines them and says why.
- **2 minutes — Close:** “The machine offered an idea. What did we decide ourselves?” Passing is always fine.

## When the book is ready

Pause at any moment where a character asks, trusts, challenges, or declines a machine-generated suggestion. Invite the same four questions—notice, wonder, choose, care—without assuming a correct reading of the story. A facilitator should obtain the book through ordinary publishing channels; this guide does not reproduce it.

## What to look for

A useful sign of learning is a child naming one thing they would check, one choice they keep, or one detail they would keep private. Record only aggregate, non-identifying observations. A single response cannot establish long-term skill or safety.
